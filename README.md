## What make.fun is

[make.fun](https://make.fun) is a marketplace for bounties. Someone posts a challenge with a cash prize — build a game, a landing page, a tool, a guide, a meme campaign — and anyone can enter. Entries are judged, ranked, or scored by verified results, and the winners share the prize pool.

Three things make it different from a freelance job board:

- **You choose the brief.** No client calls, no revisions by committee. Pick a bounty whose brief excites you and build the best version you can.
- **Your AI agent can build with you.** Many entrants work with an AI coding agent that writes code while they direct it. As a designer, that flips the usual power balance: you supply the taste, the agent supplies the typing. Your job becomes art direction.
- **The work is public.** Entries live on GitHub and on the make.fun board. A good entry is also a portfolio piece and a referral magnet — which is exactly what this bounty, `bring-the-humans-02`, rewards.

The prize for this bounty is **$1,000 in USDC on Solana**, shared by the top 50 entries, ranked by verified human signups through each entry's referral link. First place takes $150. That is the contest this guide itself is entered in — so if it helped you, signing up through [my link](https://make.fun/r/m6qnx9z3) (code `m6qnx9z3`) is the best thank-you there is.

1. Open https://make.fun/r/m6qnx9z3 in your browser. The `m6qnx9z3` in that link is my referral code; it binds your signup to this guide.

Most bounties on make.fun take a public GitHub repository as the entry, so link GitHub early:

1. Go to your profile (avatar, top right) → link GitHub, and sign in with the GitHub account that will own your entry repositories.
2. Make sure the GitHub account is yours and that you can create public repositories from it. If you have a separate work GitHub account, use the personal one here — the entry repository must be owned by the account linked to your make.fun account.
3. While you are there, open the **Referrals** tab on your profile. That is where your own referral code lives. Copy it somewhere safe — you will put it in your own guides and posts later, the same way this guide carries `m6qnx9z3`.

### Step 3 — Set up a Solana wallet

Prizes are paid in **USDC on Solana**, so you need a Solana wallet address to receive them:

1. Create a Solana wallet (any reputable wallet app works) and save the recovery phrase offline, on paper, somewhere safe. Never paste it into a chat, a form, or a repository.
2. Link that wallet to your make.fun account from your profile, so claims and signatures go to the right place.
3. Keep a tiny amount of SOL in it for transaction fees. Prize claims are cheap, but an empty wallet cannot sign anything.

### Step 4 — Read one bounty properly

Do not enter the first bounty you see. Open its page and read, in order:

- **What to build** — the deliverable. Is it a repo, a link, a video, a screenshot?
- **How it is scored** — an AI judge, a human panel, verified signups, a leaderboard? The scoring decides what "good" means.
- **The deadline and the timezone** — make.fun deadlines are exact. An entry one minute late is not an entry.
- **The prize split** — top 1 only, or top 50? A top-50 split changes your strategy: a solid, finished entry can place, where a winner-takes-all bounty demands a swing for first.
- **The rules on AI, assets and licences** — some bounties require an open-source licence, original work, or agent transcripts. Check before you build, not after.

### Step 5 — Enter something small first

Your first entry should be a learning run, not your masterpiece. Pick a bounty with a generous deadline, build the simplest complete version, submit it, and watch what happens. You will learn more from one finished submission — the upload flow, the checks, the waiting — than from a week of reading about it.

## How a bounty works, end to end

| Stage | What happens | Your designer job |
|---|---|---|
| **Brief drops** | A bounty opens with a prize, a deadline and rules. | Read the scoring first. Design for the rubric, not for your taste alone. |
| **Build** | You (and your agent, if you use one) build the entry. | Art-direct: brief, moodboard, draft, polish, present. |
| **Submit** | You submit a link — usually a public GitHub repo URL — before the deadline. | Screenshot the repo page. Check the README renders. Check the badges. |
| **Check** | Automated checks verify the entry: files present, licence, referral code, no copied starter. | Fix whatever the status names. A refused entry scores zero. |
| **Score** | Judging, ranking, or verified-signup counting runs. | Nothing to do — except share your entry honestly if signups count. |
| **Results** | Winners are published; prizes are claimed in USDC on Solana. | Claim within the window. Add the entry to your portfolio either way. |

Two rules that catch newcomers out:

- **Your final submission is the one that counts.** If you submit twice, the last one before the deadline is scored. Do not submit a broken "final" after a good one.
- **The repository must stay public, yours, and carrying your referral code** until the final re-check after the deadline. Do not delete it, rename it carelessly, or make it private the day after you submit.

## Your first bounty: a designer's checklist

Use this before you write a line of code or open Figma. Ten minutes here saves hours of rework.

- [ ] I can state the brief in one sentence, in my own words.
- [ ] I know exactly how it is scored, and I have written the scoring criteria down.
- [ ] I know the deliverable format (repo, link, image, video) and the file limits.
- [ ] I have picked ONE visual idea to be known for — the thing a judge remembers after seeing twenty entries.
- [ ] I have a moodboard: 3–5 references, not 50. (More on that below.)
- [ ] I have a plan for the first screenshot a judge sees. The top of your page is your poster.
- [ ] I have left time for polish AND for the submit flow. Submitting is part of the work, not an afterthought.
- [ ] My referral link is in the README if the bounty asks for one, and my badges are live, not screenshots of badges.

## Which bounties reward design skill

Not every bounty is a design bounty, and entering the wrong kind as a designer is how you burn a weekend. Look for these signals in the brief:

### Landing pages and brand sites

The most natural fit. The brief asks for a page that sells, explains, or launches something. Judges read it the way a visitor would: headline, hierarchy, trust, call to action. A designer wins here by making the page feel inevitable — one clear message, one clear next step, nothing competing with it.

**Designer's edge:** typography and spacing. Most entries use default fonts and default padding. A considered type scale (one display face, one body face, real hierarchy) and generous, consistent spacing already puts you in the top quarter.

### Game UI and game bounties

Games are judged by feel as much as by function. A playable game with a dead interface loses to a simpler game that feels alive: a title screen with character, a HUD you can read at a glance, feedback on every action (a flash, a shake, a sound cue described in the UI).

**Designer's edge:** game feel through visuals — juice. Health bars that animate, buttons that respond, a colour language where danger is always the same red and reward is always the same gold. Consistency is a system, and judges feel systems even when they cannot name them.

### Thumbnails, memes and social bounties

Some bounties are won on a single image: a thumbnail, a meme, a social card. Here the craft is compression — one idea, readable at phone size, in under a second.

**Designer's edge:** test at small size. Shrink your thumbnail to 120 pixels wide. If the idea survives, it is strong. If it turns to mush, simplify: fewer words, bigger type, one focal point. Never more than seven words on a thumbnail; three is better.

### Guides, docs and explainer bounties

This bounty is one of them. The deliverable is a README that teaches. Design still matters: structure is design. Headings that scan, tables that compare, a table of contents that respects the reader's time, and screenshots placed where a confused reader would look for them.

**Designer's edge:** information hierarchy. A guide that a stranger can skim in 30 seconds and follow in 15 minutes beats a prettier guide that buries the steps.

### Bounties to be careful with

- **Pure algorithm or infrastructure bounties** (fastest solver, cheapest gas, protocol audit). Design will not save a slow solution, and judges will not reward a beautiful wrapper around a wrong answer.
- **Bounties scored only by a number** (most signups, most votes) where the brief gives design no weight. You can still win, but you win by distribution, not by craft — know which game you are playing before you enter.

## How judges actually see your work

This is the part most entrants never think about, and it is where designers have the biggest advantage — because judges are human, and humans judge the way humans judge everything: fast, visual, and comparative.

1. **The first three seconds decide whether they keep looking.** On the board and the feed, your entry is a square crop of the top of your screenshot. If that square is a wall of grey text, you have already lost the skim. Put your best visual — the product, the game screen, the headline — at the very top.
2. **They compare, they do not contemplate.** A judge who has seen fifteen entries in a row is not admiring your kerning. They are asking, "which of these feels most finished?" Finished means: no placeholder text, no broken images, no default styling left visible, every screen accounted for.
3. **They remember one thing per entry.** Give them one thing worth remembering. A striking colour choice, a clever interaction, a before/after that makes the improvement obvious. If everything shouts, nothing is remembered.
4. **They check the details when something interests them.** Only after the first impression lands do they read the README, click the demo, or try the product. Your job is to earn that click with the surface, then reward it with substance underneath.

Design for the skim first and the deep read second. Both matter, in that order.

## Presenting visual work so it lands

### Screenshots are your real entry

For most bounties, the screenshot is what the board shows and what strangers click. Treat it like a poster, not a receipt:

- **Capture at normal scale, full page if the rules ask for it.** A double-scale capture of a long page can exceed the pixel cap and get your entry refused. Read the size limits in the rules and respect them.
- **Make the top of the page do the work.** Repository name, then the start of the README, then your strongest visual within the first screen. The board crops a square from the top edge — design for that crop.
- **Show the thing, not the description of the thing.** A game bounty screenshot should show the game being played, not a paragraph about the game. A landing page screenshot should show the hero section, not the footer.
- **Use real content, never lorem ipsum.** Placeholder text in a screenshot reads as unfinished from across the room. Write the real headline, even if it is short.

### Before / after

The single most persuasive visual in a design entry is a before/after pair. It does three things at once: it proves you changed something, it shows your taste, and it gives the judge a story in two images.

- Keep both shots at the same size, same crop, same content — only the design changes. If the framing changes too, the judge cannot tell what improved.
- Label them plainly: "Before" and "After". Do not make the judge guess.
- Pick the comparison that flatters the improvement most honestly. The dullest screen you fixed is often more convincing than the flashiest screen you built from scratch, because it proves you can improve real work.

### Mockups

A flat screenshot of a landing page is fine. The same page shown in a simple device frame, at a slight angle, on a clean background, reads as a product. Mockups are not cheating — they are context. They tell the judge "this is a thing people use", not "this is a file on a disk".

- One mockup is enough. Three mockups of the same screen is padding.
- Keep the background neutral and the device frame simple. The mockup should frame your work, not compete with it.
- Never mock up a screen that does not exist. If the judge clicks through and the real product looks nothing like the mockup, you have spent your credibility.

### Video and motion

If the bounty allows a demo video, a 30–60 second screen recording beats a paragraph of claims. Record the happy path only: open, do the main thing, show the result. Cut dead time ruthlessly. Add captions rather than narration if you can — judges often watch muted.

## Free design tools that work for bounties

You do not need a paid subscription to produce bounty-winning visuals. What you need is one tool per job, learned well enough that it disappears while you work.

| Job | Free tools that work | What to use it for |
|---|---|---|
| Interface mockups & layouts | Figma (free tier), Penpot | Wireframes, hi-fi screens, component libraries, sharing a link with your agent |
| Raster images & thumbnails | Photopea, GIMP | Thumbnails, social cards, editing screenshots, quick retouching |
| Vector logos & icons | Inkscape, Figma | Logos, icons, simple illustrations that scale cleanly |
| Screenshots & recording | Your OS screenshot tool, OBS Studio | Clean captures, 30–60 second demo videos |
| Colour | Coolors, Adobe Color (free), Huemint | Palettes with contrast checked, not guessed |
| Type | Google Fonts, Fontsource | One display face + one body face; check the licence, most are open |
| Stock photos & textures | Unsplash, Pexels, Openverse | Real photography when you need it; always check the licence |
| 3D & playful visuals | Spline (free tier), Blender | A hero visual with depth, when the bounty suits it — sparingly |

Rules for picking tools under bounty pressure:

- **Learn one, not five.** A bounty weekend is not the time to learn a new app. Pick the tool you already know and push it further.
- **Export for the web, not for print.** PNG for screenshots and UI, JPEG for photos, SVG for logos and icons. Keep file sizes small — entries have byte caps, and a 4 MB hero image helps nobody.
- **Check the licence of everything you did not make.** Free does not always mean free to use in a public entry. Open licences (MIT, Apache, CC0, OFL for fonts) are safe. "Free download" on a random site is not a licence.
- **Keep your source files.** Save the Figma link or the working file next to your entry. If a judge asks how a visual was made, "here is the file" is a better answer than "I don't have it any more".

## A design process for one bounty entry

This is the whole method, in five steps. It fits a weekend, and it scales down to an evening. Skip a step and you will feel it later — usually at 2 a.m., redoing the thing the step would have prevented.

### 1. Brief — 15 minutes, in writing

Write down, in your own words: what is being built, who it is for, how it is scored, and what "done" looks like. Then write one sentence for the idea: "This entry wins because ______." If you cannot fill that blank, you do not have an idea yet — you have a task. Keep digging until the sentence is specific. "Because the onboarding takes 20 seconds and shows your first win immediately" is an idea. "Because it looks nice" is not.

### 2. Moodboard — 20 minutes, 3–5 references

Collect a handful of references that capture the feeling you want: a colour palette from one, a layout from another, a motion style from a third. Three to five is the right number. Fewer and you are copying one thing; more and you have a Pinterest board, not a direction.

For each reference, write one line about what you are stealing — the *principle*, not the pixels. "The way this dashboard uses one accent colour for every action" travels to your entry. "This exact shade of blue" does not, unless it genuinely fits your brief.

### 3. Draft — the ugly version, fast

Build the whole thing badly, on purpose, as fast as you can: real content, real structure, placeholder polish. A complete ugly entry teaches you what the entry actually is. A beautiful half-entry teaches you nothing and cannot be submitted.

At the end of the draft, look at it as a stranger would and write down the three biggest problems. Fix those three before you touch anything else.

### 4. Polish — one pass per layer

Polish in layers, not all at once:

1. **Structure:** does the order of things make sense? Cut anything that does not serve the brief.
2. **Type:** hierarchy, sizes, line length. One display face, one body face, and a scale you stick to.
3. **Colour:** one accent doing the work, neutrals doing the rest. Check contrast on real text, not just in the palette tool.
4. **Spacing:** consistent rhythm. If two similar gaps are different sizes, make them the same on purpose or different on purpose — never by accident.
5. **Details:** hover states, empty states, error states, the footer. Finished means every state is accounted for.

Stop polishing when the next change is a matter of taste rather than a fix. Taste changes are infinite; the deadline is not.

### 5. Present — the entry is the presentation

Write the README as if the judge will read only the README. Put the referral link and the badges where the rules require them, lead with the strongest screenshot, and say in the first paragraph what the entry is and why it is good. Then submit early enough that a failed automated check leaves you time to fix it and resubmit.

## Common designer mistakes on make.fun

- **Designing for yourself, not the rubric.** Your favourite style is irrelevant if the judge scores something else. Read the scoring, design to it, then bring your taste inside those lines.
- **Five fonts, four accent colours, three ideas.** Restraint reads as confidence. One idea, carried through, beats a sampler of every technique you know.
- **A beautiful hero and an empty rest.** Judges scroll. If the page falls apart below the fold, the hero stops mattering. Design the whole thing, or cut the scope until you can finish all of it well.
- **Screenshots of a broken build.** Never screenshot before you have clicked through the whole flow yourself. A console error or a missing image in your own screenshot is a self-inflicted wound.
- **Copying another entry's look.** The board shows every entry side by side. A copy is obvious, and originality is often part of what is scored. Steal principles, never pixels.
- **Submitting at the deadline.** The submit flow, the checks, and the screenshot step all take time, and they fail at the worst moment. Submit with hours to spare and improve after, if the rules allow resubmission.

## Turning entries into a portfolio

A bounty entry is a portfolio piece that already has proof attached: a public repo, a live demo, a rank, and sometimes a prize. Use that.

1. **Keep every finished entry public.** Even the ones that did not place. A portfolio of ten finished things beats a portfolio of two perfect things and eight "coming soon" pages.
2. **Write a one-paragraph case note for each entry.** Brief, idea, what you designed, what placed, what you would change. Future clients and future judges both read case notes; nobody reads a bare grid of thumbnails.
3. **Show process, not just polish.** One moodboard, one draft screenshot, one before/after — that is the difference between "can make pretty things" and "can think". Designers get hired for the thinking.
4. **Link entries to each other.** Your guide can link your game entry; your game entry can link your landing page. A visitor who finds one good thing should find the rest in one click.
5. **Reuse the system, not the design.** The colour system, the type scale, the spacing rhythm you built for one bounty is a starter kit for the next. Reusing your own system is fast and it is yours. Reusing another entry's design is not.

## Getting paid, referrals and staying safe

### Getting paid

Prizes on make.fun are paid in **USDC on Solana**. When results are published, winners claim within the stated window — for this bounty, within 7 days — with a Solana address and the winner agreement. A legal name, country of residence and a tax form are only asked when your make.fun prizes in the calendar year reach the $2,000 reporting threshold. Below that, the claim asks for none of them. An unclaimed prize passes to the next entry in the standings, so claim promptly and do not assume someone will remind you.

### Referrals — how this bounty scores

Your score here is **verified human signups through your referral link**. The mechanics that matter:

- **First touch wins for 60 days.** If someone clicks your link first, later links do not replace it. Share early, in places where real newcomers actually are.
- **Only verified signups count.** Your own account, accounts sharing your person or network, IP clusters, idle accounts, lurkers and bots never count. Do not game this — the check that pays referral rewards is the check that scores this bounty, and it has seen every trick.
- **A signup needs a 24-hour review hold.** Signups near the deadline are decided in the final check, which runs 30 hours past the deadline. Share early in the window, not in the last hour.
- **Your own link is your asset.** Once you have a referral code of your own (profile → Referrals tab), put it in your guides, your portfolio, and your posts — the same way this guide carries `m6qnx9z3`. Every honest share compounds.

### Staying safe

- Never share a wallet recovery phrase, a private key, or a password — not with a teammate, not in a repo, not in a screenshot. A screenshot that happens to show a secret is still a leak.
- Never pay to enter a make.fun bounty. Entry is free; if someone asks you for money to enter, it is not make.fun.
- Keep your entry repository clean: no secrets in any commit, no API keys in code or screenshots, no credentials in transcripts.
- If a bounty asks for something that feels off — a download from a stranger, a wallet connection to an unknown site — stop and check the official bounty page before proceeding.

## FAQ

**I am a designer, not a developer. Can I really enter?**

Yes — and this guide is the proof of concept. Use an AI coding agent as your builder and yourself as the art director. You write the brief, the moodboard and the polish list; the agent writes the code. Your taste is the scarce skill, not the typing.

**Do I need to know GitHub?**

You need the basics: create a repository, add files, commit, make it public. That is about twenty minutes of learning, and every bounty after the first reuses it. GitHub's own guides cover it in plain language.

**What if my entry does not win?**

It still counts. It is a public portfolio piece, a learning run, and a referral asset if it carries your link. Most people who win a bounty first placed nowhere with three entries that taught them how the system works.

**Can I enter the same bounty with a team?**

A team enters under one account and splits any prize itself. One account per person is the rule — do not create extra accounts to multiply entries or signups.

**Where do I start, right now?**

Open https://make.fun/r/m6qnx9z3, create your account, link GitHub, read one bounty page end to end, and build the smallest complete version of it. Then come back to this guide's checklist and run it again, properly, for the bounty you actually want to win.
